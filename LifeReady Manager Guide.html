Using LifeReady with your team

A manager's guide to introducing LifeReady, built on the ADKAR change framework. It helps you support people through change without ever looking over their shoulder.

Draft v0.1 for development. Not clinical, financial or HR advice.

Read this first

LifeReady is a private reflection tool, not a management tool. Its value depends on people answering honestly about what matters to them. That only happens if they know you cannot see their answers.

Five ground rules apply throughout:

  • Voluntary. Nobody is required to use it, and nobody is asked whether they did.
  • Private by default. You never see individual results. Team-level themes appear only when enough people opt in to share (suggested minimum: 8), and only as aggregates.
  • Never used in reviews. Nothing from LifeReady informs performance, promotion, rostering or redundancy decisions.
  • You are not a counsellor. If someone shares serious distress, listen, thank them, and point to your employee assistance program or a professional. Do not try to treat it.
  • Their life, their answer. Do not steer people toward a "right" outcome, including staying in the job.

When to use this guide

  • A team is going through change (restructure, new systems, reduced hours, redundancies, mergers).
  • People are approaching a life-stage transition such as semi-retirement, returning from leave, or a change of role.
  • You want to offer a wellbeing benefit that is practical rather than a poster on the wall.

ADKAR at a glance

ADKAR describes five things a person needs, in order, for change to stick. Most rollouts fail because leaders jump straight to Ability (training and features) when people have not yet reached Desire. Diagnose where each person is, not just where the team is.

1. Awareness

People understand why this is being offered and what it is not.

Your job
  • Explain the purpose in plain terms: a private tool to help people notice what matters to them and what gets space in their lives.
  • State the privacy rules out loud, before anything else.
  • Be honest about why now, especially if it follows a difficult announcement.
You might say
"We're offering this because change is hard on more than the calendar. It's private, optional, and I can't see anything you enter."
Watch for

Suspicion that this is a disguised assessment. If you hear it, address it directly instead of defending the tool.

2. Desire

People choose to engage because it is useful to them.

Your job
  • Invite, do not push. Share your own experience if you have tried it and are comfortable doing so.
  • Connect it to something people already care about, such as time, energy or the next chapter.
  • Accept "no" without follow-up.
Conversation prompts
"If you had more room in your week, what would you put in it?"
"What part of your work gives you something you'd want to keep, whatever happens next?"
Watch for

Enthusiasm from you being read as expectation. Low take-up is not a failure.

3. Knowledge

People know what their options are and how the tool works.

Your job
  • Run a short optional walkthrough (20 minutes) covering the Life Map, how results are private, and how experiments work.
  • Explain "unbundling": work provides challenge, belonging, structure and identity, and each can be found elsewhere.
  • Share your organisation's real options (flexible hours, phased transitions, learning budgets) so reflection can lead somewhere.
Watch for

Insight with no path forward. If the organisation cannot offer any flexibility, say so plainly rather than implying otherwise.

4. Ability

People try something small in real life.

Your job
  • Protect a little time. An experiment that has to fit around a full workload will not happen.
  • Offer optional peer groups where people share experiments they choose to talk about.
  • Support workplace-linked experiments, such as mentoring, a learning project or a day of different work.
Conversation prompts
"Is there anything getting in the way of trying this that I could change?"
Watch for

Experiments becoming another task. If people fall behind, the answer is fewer, smaller experiments.

5. Reinforcement

What people learn shapes real decisions and lasts.

Your job
  • Hold regular one-to-ones about the person's next chapter, led by what they choose to bring.
  • Act on team themes you can legitimately address (workload, flexibility, learning time).
  • Celebrate what people learned, not what they completed.
Watch for

Fading after the launch push. Keep it light and regular instead of running a campaign.

How to tell if it is working

Do not measure individuals. Look at anonymous, opt-in signals only:

  • Awareness: understanding of the tool's purpose and privacy (short pulse question).
  • Desire and Ability: proportion of participants who tried at least one experiment.
  • Reinforcement: participants who say it changed a real decision, and team-level themes you acted on.
If someone is struggling. Signs include withdrawal, hopelessness or talk of not coping. Listen without judging, do not diagnose, thank them for telling you, and connect them with your employee assistance program or a health professional. In an emergency, contact emergency services.
